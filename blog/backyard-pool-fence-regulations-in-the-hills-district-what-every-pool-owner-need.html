Backyard Pool Fence Regulations in the Hills District: What Every Pool Owner Needs to Know

27 November 2026  ·  8 min read

If you have a backyard pool in the Hills District, understanding your legal obligations around fencing is just as important as maintaining the pool itself. This article breaks down the key regulations, compliance requirements, and practical steps every Hills District pool owner needs to know.

Pool Fence Regulations That Apply in the Hills District

Pool fence requirements in the Hills District are governed by a combination of New South Wales state legislation and local council rules administered by The Hills Shire Council. The primary piece of legislation is the Swimming Pools Act 1992 (NSW), along with the Swimming Pools Regulation 2018, which set the minimum standards all residential pool owners must meet regardless of when their pool was built.

The core requirements include the following:

  • Barrier height: Pool barriers must be at least 1.2 metres high, measured on the outside of the fence.
  • Non-climbable zones: A 900mm non-climbable zone must be maintained on the outside of the barrier to prevent children from gaining footholds.
  • Self-closing and self-latching gates: All gates forming part of the pool barrier must close and latch automatically, with the latch positioned on the inside of the gate at a minimum height of 1.5 metres.
  • Four-sided isolation fencing: The pool must be isolated from the house and surrounding areas by a continuous barrier on all four sides, except where compliant walls or structures form part of the barrier.
  • CPR signage: A current cardiopulmonary resuscitation sign must be displayed in a visible position near the pool.

The Hills Shire Council also requires pools to be registered on the NSW Swimming Pool Register, and properties with pools are subject to periodic council inspections. If you are selling or leasing a property with a pool, a valid compliance certificate is mandatory.

Keeping these physical boundaries secure is a core part of backyard safety, and many Hills District homeowners pair compliant pool fencing with security camera systems to maintain full visibility of the pool area at all times.

Height, Material and Gate Requirements for Residential Pool Fencing

Understanding the specific technical requirements for pool fencing is essential for any Hills District homeowner. New South Wales law, governed by the Swimming Pools Act 1992 and the Building Code of Australia, sets out clear minimum standards that all residential pool barriers must meet.

  • Minimum fence height: Pool barriers must be at least 1.2 metres tall, measured on the outside (non-pool) side. This applies to all four sides of the barrier surrounding the pool area.
  • Climbable footholds: No horizontal rails, fittings or objects that could act as a foothold must be present on the outside face of the fence within the 1.2 metre height zone. This rules out many traditional timber paling designs unless they are specifically configured to comply.
  • Acceptable materials: Common compliant options include tubular aluminium, glass panels (frameless or semi-frameless), powder-coated steel, and certain treated timber designs. Each material must still meet the height and climbability rules.
  • Gap restrictions: Vertical gaps between fence members must be no greater than 100 mm. The gap between the bottom of the fence and the ground must also be no more than 100 mm.

Gates present some of the most common compliance failures inspectors find during Hills District pool audits. All gates must:

  • Open away from the pool (outward-swinging only)
  • Be fitted with a self-closing mechanism that functions from any position
  • Include a self-latching lock positioned on the pool side of the gate, at least 1.5 metres above ground, or recessed so a child cannot reach through to operate it

Much like a professionally installed CCTV system, a compliant pool fence is only as reliable as its weakest component. Regular checks of hinges, latches and gate alignment can prevent both safety incidents and costly compliance notices from Hills Shire Council.

Council Rules and Approval Processes in the Hills Shire

The Hills Shire Council administers pool fence compliance in line with the Swimming Pools Act 1992 (NSW) and its accompanying regulation, but the council also applies its own local development controls that homeowners must navigate carefully. Before you install or modify a pool barrier, understanding the approval process can save you significant time and money.

In most cases, a compliant pool fence in the Hills District will fall under exempt development, meaning no formal development application (DA) is required, provided the barrier meets all specified standards. However, certain situations will trigger the need for a formal approval, including:

  • Heritage-listed properties: Any fence or barrier work near a heritage item requires council assessment before construction begins.
  • Boundary fence arrangements: Where a pool fence doubles as a boundary fence shared with a neighbour, additional council considerations apply.
  • Non-standard materials or configurations: Glass, cable or custom barrier systems may require a complying development certificate (CDC) or DA.
  • Proximity to easements or drainage corridors: Common in many Hills Shire properties, these can affect fence placement and trigger a review.

Once your pool barrier is installed, Hills Shire Council requires the pool to be registered on the NSW Swimming Pool Register and inspected by a council officer or accredited certifier. Properties being sold or leased must hold a valid compliance certificate, so keeping your paperwork current is essential.

Common Pool Fence Compliance Failures in the Hills District (And How to Avoid Them)

Even well-intentioned homeowners can find themselves failing a pool fence inspection. Understanding the most frequent compliance issues in the Hills District can save you significant time, money and stress when your certifier comes to check the barrier.

  • Gap sizes exceeding allowable limits: Any opening in a pool barrier must not allow a 100 mm sphere to pass through. Over time, timber palings can warp or shrink, creating gaps that were not there at the time of original installation. Regular checks of your fence panels can catch this early.
  • Non-compliant self-closing gates: Pool gates must swing away from the pool area and close automatically from any open position. A gate that fails to latch after being released even slightly is an immediate failure. Lubricate and test your gate mechanism every few months.
  • Climbable objects within 900 mm of the fence: Garden furniture, plant pots, retaining walls and even large rocks placed close to the barrier can give a child a foothold. Keep a clear exclusion zone on the outside of the fence at all times.
  • Doors and windows used as part of the barrier: Windows and doors that form part of the pool barrier must be fitted with compliant safety mechanisms. Standard fly screens do not qualify, and many inspections fail on this point alone.
  • Incorrect fence height: The minimum height for a pool barrier in NSW is 1.2 metres, measured from the finished ground level on the outside. Settling soil or added garden beds can reduce the effective height over time.

A practical tip for Hills District homeowners is to conduct a visual inspection of your pool barrier every season. Changes in weather, landscaping work and general wear can introduce new hazards between formal inspections. Pairing a physical barrier audit with a monitored security camera covering the pool zone adds a valuable extra layer of oversight, particularly during hours when the yard is unattended.

Inspections, Certificates and Ongoing Maintenance Obligations

Once your pool fence is installed in the Hills District, your compliance journey is far from over. New South Wales law requires all pool owners to hold a valid compliance certificate, and local councils conduct periodic inspections to confirm that barriers remain up to standard. Here is what you need to know about keeping everything above board.

  • Initial inspection: After installation, a council inspector or accredited certifier must assess the barrier before the pool is used. A certificate of compliance is issued only when every element meets the Swimming Pools Act 1992 requirements.
  • Three-year inspection cycle: Pools that are shared holiday rental properties or located in strata schemes must be inspected every three years. Hills Shire Council can request inspections for other residential pools at any time.
  • Register your pool: All pools must be listed on the NSW Swimming Pool Register. Failure to register can result in fines and complications when selling or leasing your property.
  • Ongoing maintenance checks: Gates must self-close and self-latch consistently. Hinges, latches and fence panels should be inspected seasonally for rust, warping or impact damage that could compromise the barrier height or integrity.
  • Vegetation and clutter: Plants, furniture and equipment stored near the fence can create climbing aids. Clear these regularly to maintain compliant clearances on both sides of the barrier.

Many Hills District homeowners choose to combine their pool fence maintenance routine with a broader property security review, checking outdoor lighting, camera coverage and sensor placement at the same time. This integrated approach makes it easier to spot vulnerabilities before they become costly problems.

Staying compliant with backyard pool fence regulations in the Hills District demands attention at every stage, from design and council approval through to routine upkeep and periodic re-inspection. By understanding barrier standards, engaging qualified certifiers and scheduling regular maintenance checks, Hills District homeowners can protect their families, avoid penalties and enjoy their pools with genuine peace of mind all year round.
